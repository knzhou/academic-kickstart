## Axion dark matter searches

Axion dark matter can drive extremely weak electromagnetic signals. I develop ways to amplify these signals and make new regions of axion parameter space accessible. In the heterodyne approach, axions convert an excited cavity mode into a second mode at a slightly different frequency. This allows low-mass axions to be searched for using high-frequency superconducting cavities. My work spans the original detection proposals, broadband search strategies, and the design and testing of a prototype cavity. I also study polarization haloscopes, which use oscillating nuclear electric dipole moments to probe the axion's coupling to QCD.

## Spin-dependent dark matter

Dark matter could reveal itself through forces and torques on electron spins. I study the resulting signals in materials and how they can guide new experiments, from magnetized multilayers for axion detection to searches for ultralight axial vectors. A useful connection is that neutrons also couple to electron spin: existing neutron scattering measurements can therefore predict the response of a dark matter detector, helping identify promising target materials without relying solely on microscopic calculations.

## Quantum effects and precision measurements

When do experiments need a quantum description, and when is a classical picture enough? I investigate this question for axion dark matter, gravitational waves, and precision measurements of the electron's magnetic moment. For weakly coupled waves, detector averaging and weak coupling strongly suppress intrinsically quantum signatures. In electron magnetic moment experiments, a quantum calculation of the cavity's effect agrees with the classical result and provides a way to account for additional systematic effects. I also study how rapidly oscillating dark matter fields can produce a steady, second-order response.

## Continuous-spin fields

Relativity and quantum mechanics allow massless particles with a continuous spin scale, a possibility beyond the familiar fixed-helicity photon and graviton. I study how these fields can interact with matter and what their observable consequences would be. Our framework recovers familiar forces at frequencies above the spin scale, while predicting deviations at low frequencies and long distances. These include changes to the Lorentz force and to radiation from an accelerating charge, opening a route to experimental tests of this broader class of theories.
